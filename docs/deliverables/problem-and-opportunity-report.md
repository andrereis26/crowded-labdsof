# Problem and Opportunity Report

## 1. Target Community

### 1.1 Event Attendees

People who attend cultural and public events in a city, including:

- Concerts
- Festivals
- Theatre and shows
- Popular festivities (e.g., Santos Populares)
- Municipal and community events
- Sports events

For paid events, attendees usually buy their tickets on external ticketing platforms (e.g., Ticketline, BOL). For free public events, there is often no ticket at all. In both cases, once they have decided to attend, they rely on multiple channels to learn about changes and on-site conditions.

### 1.2 Event Organizers

Promoters, venues, cultural associations and public entities (e.g., municipalities, parish councils) that need better tools to:

- Test interest in an event before booking it (whether to bring an artist, for how many nights, in which room)
- Plan capacity based on expected attendance
- Communicate event changes to their audience
- Publish on-site conditions such as crowd levels and queues
- Send operational alerts during the event
- Receive and respond to on-site incidents

---

## 2. Problem Statement

Event participants frequently face disruptions — cancellations, schedule or venue changes, overcrowding, long queues and on-site incidents — and learn about them too late.

Before the event, changes are communicated across multiple channels:

- Ticketing platforms
- Organizer websites
- Social media platforms
- Email

During the event, participants have little or no visibility into:

- Crowd levels
- Queue lengths
- Closed entrances or access changes
- On-site incidents

Event organizers also face significant uncertainty when deciding whether and how big to book an event and when planning capacity, and lack a direct and fast channel to reach the people who are on their way to, or already at, the event.

> **As a result, both participants and organizers react to disruptions based on incomplete or outdated information, leading to wasted trips, overcrowding, avoidable queues and slow incident response.**

### 2.1 The Disruption Lifecycle

Crowded addresses four of the disruption types listed in the assignment: **changes to scheduled activities** (cancellations, postponements, venue, time and doors opening changes), **community events and crowd-management situations** (overcrowding, queues, closed entrances), **accessibility barriers** reported on site, and **extreme weather events** that affect an event.

Every disruption goes through three phases. Today, each phase fails for a different reason:

| Phase | Typical disruptions | Who knows first | How people find out today | What is missing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Anticipate** (before leaving home) | Cancellation, postponement, venue or time change, doors opening later, severe weather | Ticketing source, organizer, weather service | Ticketing email, social media, organizer website, or not at all | One place that collects every change for the events a person attends, in time to change plans |
| **Respond** (on the way and on site) | Overcrowding, long queues, closed entrance, capacity reached, on-site incident | Organizer and the people already at the venue | On-site staff, word of mouth, social media posts | A direct channel from the organizer to everyone on the way or on site, and a way for participants to report what they see |
| **Recover** (after the disruption) | Rescheduled date, refund, entrance reopened, incident resolved | Ticketing source and organizer | Separate emails or nothing; users must search for what changes for them | A clear message on what changes for the participant (new date, ticket validity, refund) and confirmation that the situation is over |

In Portugal, a cancelled concert, or one moved to another date, time or venue, gives the right to a refund, which must be paid within 30 days (see 3.6). Participants still have to find out that the event changed before they can act on it.

## 3. Problem Evidence

### 3.1 Fragmented Communication of Changes

When an event is changed, postponed, relocated or cancelled, communication is frequently dispersed across ticketing platforms, social media, organizer websites and email.

Users must actively monitor several channels to stay informed, and may miss important updates or receive them too late. Our user research found a documented case of a user who did not receive alerts despite having notifications enabled (see User Research).

### 3.2 Ticketing Platforms Stop at the Purchase

Ticketing and discovery platforms focus on selling tickets and promoting events, but offer limited support once the ticket has been bought.

Our user research also found a documented case of a user who had difficulty accessing their tickets while travelling to a concert (see User Research). For free public events, there is no ticketing relationship at all, so there is no channel to reach attendees.

### 3.3 Lack of Real-Time On-Site Awareness

Existing platforms offer limited visibility into real-time conditions such as:

- Venue congestion
- Queue lengths
- Closed entrances
- Current attendance levels

Users often discover these conditions only after arriving at the venue.

### 3.4 Limited Capacity Planning

Event organizers frequently have difficulty estimating how many people will actually attend. The uncertainty starts before the event exists: a venue programmer deciding whether to bring an artist, whether to book one night or two, or a small or large room, often relies on intuition and on social media reactions, which do not say how many people would actually come.

This uncertainty may result in:

- Undersized staff or entrances
- Oversized venues
- Excess operational costs
- Overcrowding and poor attendee experience
- Events moved to another room or date after tickets are sold, which is itself a disruption for participants

### 3.5 No Direct Organizer-to-Audience Channel During Events

When something goes wrong on site (e.g., an entrance closes, capacity is reached, a weather warning is issued), organizers depend on social media posts or on-site staff to redirect the audience.

Participants who are on their way, or in another area of the venue, may not receive this information in time.

### 3.6 Documented Disruptions in Portugal and Europe

Recent cases show that disruptions at events are frequent and that the main failure is often how and when people are informed:

| Case | Disruption | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Megadeth at the Evil Live festival, MEO Arena, Lisbon (July 2026) | Concert cancelled shortly after its scheduled start time, citing technical problems | The audience was already at the venue when the show was called off; the explanation came through the band's social media | [Correio da Manhã, 6 July 2026](https://www.cmjornal.pt/cultura/detalhe/megadeth-cancelam-concerto-no-meo-arena-apos-hora-prevista-para-comecar) |
| North Festival, Maia (June 2026) | Overcrowding, queues of over an hour for food and around 40 minutes for drinks | Nearly a hundred complaints on Portal da Queixa; the mandatory prepaid card was only made clear on site, after the queues | [Echo Boomer, 14 June 2026](https://echoboomer.pt/north-festival-dezenas-reclamacoes-portal-da-queixa/) |
| Rock in Rio Lisboa (June 2024) | Access problems, overcrowding and queues | Complaints rose 97% compared with 2022; about 15.5% of them mention access, overcrowding and queues | [Público, 19 June 2024](https://www.publico.pt/2024/06/19/culturaipsilon/noticia/queixas-rock-in-rio-lisboa-aumentam-97-relacao-ano-passado-2094552) |

When a concert is cancelled or changed in Portugal, the consumer is entitled to a refund regardless of the reason, paid within 30 days; complaints go to the promoter and then to the IGAC ([DECO](https://deco.pt/direitos-dos-consumidores/concerto-cancelado-ou-alterado-peca-o-reembolso/)).

These cases come from news reports and complaint platforms, so they show the most visible disruptions and the view of those who complained; they do not measure how often disruptions happen across all events. Crowded does not prevent these disruptions; it shortens the time between a disruption and the moment the people affected know about it and what to do next.

## 4. Stakeholder Map

### 4.1 Event Attendees

#### Interests

- Knowing about event changes before leaving home
- Avoiding overcrowding and long queues
- Having the information about their events in one place
- Reporting problems on site

#### Benefits

- Event change notifications
- Their tickets and public events in one place, connected with the ticket or an access code
- Weather warnings for the event's district
- Crowd and queue information with its source and last-update time
- Reporting what they see (crowd level, queues, incidents), routed to the organization
- Participant estimates confirmed by other participants, showing how many confirmed them, when the organization publishes nothing

### 4.2 Event Organizers

#### Interests

- An app for their audience without building and running one
- Planning capacity
- Communicating changes and alerts quickly
- Managing crowds and queues
- Responding to incidents

#### Benefits

- A backoffice to register events, publish changes and publish on-site conditions
- Surveys for the participants connected to the event
- Direct alerts to every participant connected to the event
- Incident inbox
- AI-drafted alerts for review

### 4.3 Public Entities and Cultural Institutions

#### Interests

- Public safety at public events
- Improving visitor satisfaction
- Verified institutional communication

#### Benefits

- A verified channel to communicate with attendees
- Visibility of on-site conditions and incidents
- Better coordination during large public events

### 4.4 Ticketing Platforms (External)

#### Interests

- Reducing support requests caused by event changes
- Keeping their customers informed

#### Benefits

- Ticket holders informed of changes by the organization, which reduces the questions that reach the ticketing platform
- No competition for ticket sales: Crowded does not sell, resell or link to tickets, and only knows about a ticket when the participant imports it

## 5. Assumptions

Each assumption is something the product depends on but we have not yet proven. Each one has a way to test it during the course or right after the MVP, and a plan if it turns out false, so no single assumption can sink the product. They are reviewed at each sprint review. The professors' feedback and the usability tests of Sprint 3 are the first evidence.

| ID | Assumption | Description | How and when we validate it | If it proves false |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | **Users Want Event Changes in a Single Place** | We assume users value receiving changes for all the events they attend in one place, regardless of where the ticket was bought. | Usability tests of the participant's events with the personas' tasks (Sprint 3); after the MVP, share of participants with events from more than one organization. | Crowded becomes, for each participant, the app of one festival, which is still the product an organization pays for. |
| **A2** | **Organizations Will Adopt Crowded Instead of Their Own App** | We assume festivals and venues will pay for Crowded instead of building their own app, and will register each event and keep its changes, crowd levels, queues and alerts current if the backoffice is simple. | Interviews with two or three venue or festival organizers before the end of Sprint 3, showing them the backoffice and the dashboard (#43). | Risk R1: without organizations there are no events in Crowded. The product would target larger festivals that already pay for an app, as a cheaper replacement. |
| **A4** | **Users Are Willing to Import Tickets** | We assume participants are willing to import tickets bought elsewhere if the process is fast and gives them access to change notifications and on-site information. | Share of tickets matched from the extracted event code and time to import in the AI evaluation (#72); usability test of the import flow. | The organization gives out an access code for ticketed events too, as for public events. |
| **A5** | **Event Disruptions Are Common Enough to Justify the Product** | We assume changes, overcrowding and on-site incidents occur frequently enough to justify a dedicated communication service. | Already supported by documented cases (section 3.6); after the MVP, changes, alerts and warnings per event. | The product shifts weight to on-site information (crowds, queues, alerts), which happens at every event. |
| **A6** | **Tickets Carry a Readable Event Code and QR Code** | We assume the tickets of the organizations' events print an event code and a QR code that extraction and decoding can read from a PDF or a screenshot. | Tickets from Portuguese platforms in the AI evaluation dataset (#72). | The organization gives out an access code for ticketed events too, as for public events. |
| **A7** | **Participants Will Report What They See On Site** | We assume participants will report incidents (e.g., lost and found, accessibility barriers) if reporting is fast and the organization responds. | Usability test of reporting (#53) on the demonstration; after the MVP, reports per connected participant per event. | Reporting stays limited to the organization's own staff. |
| **A8** | **Participants Will Confirm Other Participants' Reports** | We assume participants connected to an event will answer a single-tap "Yes" or "No" confirmation about a report (e.g., "Is it very crowded?") if they are asked rarely, and that these answers are reliable enough to estimate on-site conditions when the organization publishes none. | Usability test of the single-tap confirmation (#76); after the MVP, answer rate of confirmation prompts and agreement with the organization's own data. | Estimates rely on the organization's data; the confidence rules (FR-058) show fewer estimates. |
| **A9** | **Participants Will Allow Location to Report** | We assume participants are willing to allow a one-time approximate location check when reporting or confirming, if Crowded explains that it is only used to ensure better report quality and does not store it. | Usability test of the location prompt and its explanation (FR-064); after the MVP, share of users who allow it. | Reports in non-personal categories become unavailable to those users, which lowers the volume of estimates; personal categories still work. |

## 6. Risks

### R1 — Organizer Adoption

An event exists in Crowded only when its organization registers it. Without organizations, Crowded has nothing to show; with an organization that publishes little, it shows only official changes and weather warnings.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Sell Crowded as the organization's own app, cheaper than building one, with a free app for participants
    - Keep the backoffice extremely simple
    - Weather warnings reach participants without any action by the organization
    - Participant reports confirmed by other participants provide estimates of crowd levels and queues when the organization publishes none

---

### R2 — Stale or Inaccurate Organizer Data

Organizations may not keep on-site information up to date during the event.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Always show the source and last-update time
    - Hide or flag information that has not been updated recently

---

### R3 — Availability of External Data

The weather and transport sources (IPMA, a GTFS feed) can be unavailable, late or return invalid data.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Keep each source behind an adapter with a fake for tests
    - Show the last valid data with its time when the source is unavailable
    - Reject and log invalid data

---

### R4 — False or Misleading Alerts

False cancellations or safety alerts could cause confusion or panic.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Only members of organizations created by a platform administrator can publish official information
    - Keep an audit log of changes and alerts
    - AI-generated drafts always require organizer approval

---

### R5 — Privacy and Data Security Concerns

Tickets contain personal data and access codes, and users may be hesitant to share them.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Store only the minimum ticket data needed
    - Never store access codes in plain text
    - Do not make participant identity public in reports or confirmations
    - Use approximate location, only with consent
    - For reporting and confirmations, check location once at the moment of the action and store only the result, never the location

---

### R6 — Low Participant Adoption

Without participants connected to an event, organizer alerts have little reach.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Make importing a ticket a single upload, with no review step
    - Let organizers promote public events through a QR code and an access code at the venue and online
    - The organization tells its own audience to install the app, because it is the festival's app

---

### R7 — Unreliable or Abusive Participant Reports

Participant reports may be wrong, outdated or deliberately false (e.g., trolling), and confirmation prompts may annoy users if they are too frequent.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Show a report on the event page only after other participants confirm it (Medium or High confidence)
    - Let confidence decay over time and always label participant information as an estimate that may not reflect reality
    - Show crowd and queue estimates only when the organization has no current value
    - Allow reporting (except personal categories) and confirmations only from users whose approximate location is near the venue
    - Ask only a small sample of connected users, cap how often each user is asked, and let users turn prompts off per event
    - Rate-limit reports and confirmation answers; after the MVP, automatically warn, temporarily restrict and ban from reporting users whose reports are repeatedly unhelpful

---

### R8 — Missed Critical Notifications

Users can turn off every notification type, including official event changes and operational alerts, and may then miss a cancellation or a safety instruction.

**Mitigation**

>
    - Turn every notification type on by default, both overall and for each event
    - Ask for confirmation, with a warning, before official changes or operational alerts are turned off
    - Keep official changes and alerts visible in the participant's events and on the event page even when their notifications are off

---
